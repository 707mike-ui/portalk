Settlements in the Marienwerder (Kwidzyn) Vicinity
"Mennonite settlements in the Marienwerder lowlands were to some extent the product of deliberate policy on the part of the government of Prussia and of King Frederick William I, who was determined to expand his army. The "soldier king" wanted tall Mennonite men to serve in his army, and when they refused, he ordered the expulsion of Mennonites from his realm in 1724. The expelled Mennonites now moved into Royal Prussia, to villages such as Tragheimerweide (Barcice) and Rudnerweide (Rudniki). Other villages, such as Montauerweide (Matawskie Pastwiska) and Zieglershuben also now gained numerous Mennonite settlers from Prussia.

"This resettlement process in the lowlands west of Marienwerder owed much to Jacob Fadenrecht, a dike supervisor in the Large Werder. He and an associate sold land to the religious refugees for 2,200 Prussian guilder.

"Farmers from the delta had used the land for cattle grazing. Two leaders among the refugees, Salomon Becker and Jakob Jantzen, arranged the purchase of land where the community later known as Tragheimerweide arose. Other villages in this vicinity were also established at approximately this time. Most of the villagers were Mennonites. For example, all but one of the eleven farmers in Schweingrube (Szwedy) and seventeen of eighteen in Rudnerweide were Mennonites. The villagers were mostly farmers, but a number of them also engaged in crafts, especially linen-weaving.

"Apparently, the newcomers also brought a new architectural style to the region. The Mennonites from Prussia introduced the half-timbered, arcaded houses to this area. These houses were often joined to the hay-mow and barn, and were constructed on the plots of land held by individual farmers. The familiar one-street village, with houses on both sides of the road and the land surrounding the village, was not characteristic of settlements here; Mennonite dwellings were more scattered. Mennonites had introduced a similar pattern in the delta earlier. Other settlements were established, including one on the island Quiche (Kuchnia) in the Vistula. Here the earliest settlers were all Mennonites.

"Evidently local ecclesiastical authorities were satisfied with the contributions Mennonites made to the community, for in 1728 Bishop Kretkowski of Kulm granted them permission to build their own church, which they renovated and enlarged in 1763. A century later, in 1866 the congregation built a new brick church, and in 1892, installed an organ. The church survived until the end of World War II."
